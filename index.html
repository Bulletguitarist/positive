The Four Waves of Psychology

Psychology has shifted across four big paradigms over roughly the last 150 years.

1st Wave — Disease Model / Psychoanalysis

Pre-WWII to mid-20th century. Focused on pathology, mental illness, neurosis, and curing suffering.

Seligman's 3 criticisms of the disease model:

  1. Pathologising human nature — people treated as passive victims of trauma or drives, with no room for agency.
  2. Neglect of normal functioning — talents, fulfilment, and potential were ignored.
  3. Repair over building — the field focused on fixing damage, not building positive states.

2nd Wave — Behaviourism

Early–mid 20th century — B. F. Skinner, Ivan Pavlov, John B. Watson. Psychology should measure only observable behaviour.

  • Behaviour shaped by operant & classical conditioning.
  • Dismissed internal mental states and free will as unscientific.

3rd Wave — Humanistic Psychology

1950s–60s, the "third force" — Abraham Maslow and Carl Rogers.

  • A rebound against determinism: free choice, self-actualisation, personal growth.
  • Drawback: philosophically rich but lacked rigorous empirical testing.

4th Wave — Modern Positive Psychology

1998–present, launched by Martin Seligman's 1998 APA presidential address.

  • Equal weight to flourishing, optimism, and strengths — not just illness.
  • Combines humanism's outlook with rigorous scientific method.

Founding Pioneers

William James (1842–1910)

America's first positive psychologist. In The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902), argued that optimal human potential was key to understanding the mind.

Abraham Maslow (1908–1970)

Coined "Positive Psychology" as a chapter heading in Motivation and Personality (1954). Built the Hierarchy of Needs, topped by Self-Actualisation.

Martin Seligman (1942–present)

Moved from Learned Helplessness to Learned Optimism and PERMA. The father of modern positive psychology.

Christopher Peterson (1950–2012)

Co-authored the VIA Classification with Seligman. Summed up the field in three words: "Other people matter."

East vs. West

Culture shapes how we conceptualise optimal functioning and life satisfaction.

Western Paradigm

  • Individualism, autonomy, self-esteem, personal success.
  • Linear thinking: goals → action → obstacles → progress.
  • Future-oriented: hope, optimism, planning.
  • Happiness via mastery, self-expression, achievement.

Eastern Paradigm

  • Collectivism, social harmony, interconnectedness, duty.
  • Circular thinking: balance, ending suffering.
  • Present-moment awareness with respect for tradition.
  • Happiness via inner peace, self-control, adaptability.

Traditions in Brief

  • Athenian Aristotle's Eudaimonia — happiness through virtue: courage, temperance, generosity, justice, truthfulness.
  • Judaism & Christianity divine order, faith, charity, forgiveness; the Seven Heavenly Virtues.
  • Islam Gratitude (Shukr), Patience (Sabr), Charity (Zakat), brotherhood.
  • Confucianism five core virtues — Jen (benevolence), Yi (righteousness), Li (propriety), Zhi (wisdom), Xin (sincerity).
  • Taoism the Tao and Yin & Yang — balance of opposites.
  • Buddhism suffering (Dukkha), the Four Noble Truths, the Eightfold Path, and the 4 Brahma Viharas: Maitri, Karuna, Mudita, Upeksa.
  • Hinduism interconnectedness (Brahman), self-realisation (Atman), Karma, and Moksha.

Seligman's PERMA Model (2011)

From Flourish — five measurable, independent pillars of well-being:

  • P — Positive Emotion: joy, warmth, hope, gratitude.
  • E — Engagement: full absorption, flow, time disappears.
  • R — Relationships: deep connections. "Other people matter."
  • M — Meaning: belonging to something bigger than yourself.
  • A — Accomplishment: mastery and goal attainment.

Csikszentmihalyi's Flow Theory

A state of optimal experience and intense focus, from Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi.

Conditions for Flow:

  1. Challenge–skill balance: too much challenge = anxiety; too little = boredom.
  2. Clear, immediate feedback and unambiguous goals.
  3. Merging of action and awareness.
  4. Distorted sense of time.

Fredrickson's Broaden-and-Build Theory

Barbara Fredrickson's account of why positive emotions evolved.

  • Negative emotions (fear, anger) narrow us toward fight-or-flight.
  • Positive emotions (joy, interest, love) work differently:
    1. Broaden — expand awareness, creativity, exploration.
    2. Build — durable resources over time: resilience, skills, bonds.

VIA Classification — 6 Virtues, 24 Strengths

Created by Seligman & Peterson as a positive counterweight to the DSM.

Wisdom & Knowledge: Creativity, Curiosity, Judgment, Love of Learning, Perspective.

Courage: Bravery, Perseverance, Honesty, Zest.

Humanity: Love, Kindness, Social Intelligence.

Justice: Teamwork, Fairness, Leadership.

Temperance: Forgiveness, Humility, Prudence, Self-Regulation.

Transcendence: Beauty, Gratitude, Hope, Humour, Spirituality.

Models of Well-Being

Subjective Well-Being (Ed Diener)

The scientific stand-in for "happiness" — three parts:

  • High Positive Affect
  • Low Negative Affect (distress, anger, sadness)
  • High Life Satisfaction

Psychological Well-Being (Carol Ryff)

Eudaimonic fulfilment across 6 dimensions:

  1. Autonomy — independence & self-determination.
  2. Personal Growth — development & openness.
  3. Purpose in Life — clear goals and meaning.
  4. Self-Acceptance — positive self-attitude.
  5. Environmental Mastery — managing complex circumstances.
  6. Positive Relations — deep, trusting bonds.

Lyubomirsky's Happiness Pie

  • 50% Genetic Set-Point — inherited baseline traits.
  • 10% Life Circumstances — wealth, geography, income (Hedonic Treadmill).
  • 40% Intentional Activities — gratitude, exercise, relationships, goals!

Lifestyle Interventions for Flourishing

  • Exercise raises BDNF, boosts endorphins/dopamine, cuts depression & anxiety.
  • Nutrition reduces inflammation, supports gut-made serotonin.
  • Sleep — 7–9 hours regulates the amygdala & consolidates memory.
  • Mindfulness thickens the prefrontal cortex, shrinks the amygdala.
